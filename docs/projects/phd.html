PhD research · Leeds

Making medical images useful

Turn enormous images into tractable, explainable AI.

Digital pathology creates extraordinarily detailed images. My doctoral research developed a multi-scale pipeline for learning from lymphoma tissue: organise the data, reduce its cost, compare useful representations and inspect what the models learn.

Scientific findings and where the numbers come from

The thesis investigates IHC grading against supplied expert scores and cell-of-origin classification against supplied gene-expression labels for diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.

IHC grading achieved MAE 0.238 against averaged expert scores. UNI patch features reached 97.0% accuracy and AUC 0.992 in the evaluated data. The thesis identifies non-independent internal validation: these are research results, not independently validated diagnostic performance or evidence of surpassing pathologists.

Source: Multi-Scale and Multi-Stage Deep Learning for the Classification of Diffuse Large B-cell Lymphoma Histology, Samuel Llanwarne, University of Leeds, June 2026. Printed p70, Table 2.5: extraction; pp71–72: storage; p186: scale; p161, Table 4.4: subtype results; p190: validation limitations.

The aim

Investigate how deep learning can predict supplied expert scores and lymphoma subtype labels from stained tissue images, while making the data practical to process.

The work

I developed image-preparation and labelling tools, structured cell-, patch- and core-level datasets, compared autoencoder and contrastive representations, and investigated model explanations including Spectral Dream Dimming.

What exists

The thesis reports extraction time reduced from 6 to 3 seconds per sample, patch storage reduced from 26.94 to 3.63 GB, and processed views covering 1,109 cores, 229,683 patches and 9,126,415 cell instances.

The useful insight

Useful AI starts before the model: preserve label integrity, choose representations that fit the task, and spend computation where it adds evidence.
