The scientific question

Mapping the Milky Way

We made preliminary sky maps and explored how radio observations could help us study the Milky Way’s structure, rotation and mass. The telescope’s behaviour and the quality of the data set limits on the analysis.

Project record and scope

The recovered five-slide Durham Radio Telescope Project presentation records hardware setup, preliminary sky maps, data visualisations, data cleaning and automation. It also describes enjoyment of sustained research and building explanations by following useful questions.

The presentation uses “we”; these are project activities rather than a claim of sole ownership of the telescope or every component. Samuel describes this as his Durham master’s project involving a student-built mesh telescope.

Investigating noise sources, hardware improvements and complete automation appear as future work. No numerical estimate of galactic mass, map resolution or measured performance improvement is claimed here.

The aim

Explore Milky Way structure through radio observations, connecting practical telescope work with questions about galactic rotation and mass.

The work

The project combined hardware setup and investigation with programming. Loading, cleaning, visualising and interpreting observations formed a substantial part of the work, alongside automating repeated analysis.

What exists

The project produced preliminary sky maps and data visualisations. My retrospective presentation records the hardware work, data-processing activities and scientific aims, with instrument noise and further automation identified as directions for improvement.

What I learnt

Understanding the telescope mattered as much as writing the analysis. Noise and the way we processed the observations affected what we could learn from them.
